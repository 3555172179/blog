After 2 years of B2B sales, I've sent thousands of cold emails. The first three lines decide whether the recipient reads further. So those three lines must **not feel like a template**.

## 5 opening templates

### 1. Pain-point hook (for B2B tools)
> I noticed your team has been posting [X] jobs for [specific role] — managing [X] at [scale] usually doesn't work with [existing tool]; it's a sign your current workflow is breaking down.

**Why it works**: It hits the role they're hiring for = you understand their current pain.

### 2. Data reference (universal B2B)
> Your competitor [name] just launched [X], and their traffic jumped 30% in 2 weeks per SimilarWeb. Here is what's working for them and how you could do the same.

**Why it works**: Third-party data replaces self-promotion. They infer you can do the same.

### 3. Self-intro (for niche industries)
> I'm [name], I work with [N] [industry] brands on [problem], including [client name] (who 3x'd their [metric] last quarter).

**Why it works**: Specific client as social proof beats "industry-leading" 100x over.

### 4. Referral-based (for cold start)
> [Mutual contact] suggested I reach out. We helped their team [specific result], and I think the same approach could work for you.

**Why it works**: Borrowing a referral drops psychological resistance by ~70%.

### 5. Value-first (for warm leads)
> I put together a teardown of [their product/website/marketing] — 3 quick wins you can ship this week. Sharing it in case it's useful.

**Why it works**: Give value first. They can't ignore you. Even if they don't reply, the +50 impression stays for next contact.

## Anti-patterns (never write these)

- ❌ "Hope this email finds you well."
- ❌ "We are a leading manufacturer with 20 years experience..."
- ❌ "Our product is high quality and competitive..."

Gmail auto-classifies these as bulk with 90% probability.
