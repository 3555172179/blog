The word "revert" stopped me mid-scroll. A supplier had written "I will revert to you by Friday," and I sat there wondering if they planned to turn back into an earlier version of themselves. What they meant was "reply." The email was otherwise fine, but that single word made me read the rest with suspicion. I started keeping a mental list of these small errors after that, not because I enjoy correcting people, but because I have made every one of them myself and paid for it in delayed responses and confused clients.

## The ones that actually cost you

"Revert" is the classic. In Indian and Southeast Asian business English it means "get back to you," but to American or British readers it sounds like a software command. I once wrote "I will revert soon" to a buyer in Chicago. He replied, "Revert to what?" and the thread went sideways for two days while I explained. Now I write "I will reply by Friday" or "I will get back to you."

"Kindly" is another trap. It is not wrong, but it carries a tone of irritation in American English. "Kindly send the invoice" reads like you are already annoyed. "Please send the invoice" is neutral. I learned this the hard way when a client asked if I was upset. I was not. The word just made me sound that way.

Then there is "dear" with no name. "Dear Sir/Madam" or worse, "Dear Sir," tells the reader you did not bother to find out who they are. In trade correspondence this matters because the person you are writing to often decides whether your quote gets opened or archived. I have a saved folder of templates now, and every one starts with a real name or at minimum "Hello," which is safe and modern.

## Grammar slips that change meaning

Subject line errors are the quiet killers. I sent an email with the subject "Shipment delay" when I meant to ask about a delay on the buyer's side. They read it as me announcing my own delay and started looking for backup suppliers. Two words, one misunderstanding, and I spent a week rebuilding trust. Now my subject lines include a verb: "Question about shipment delay" or "Update on shipment delay."

Prepositions with "discuss" trip people up constantly. You do not "discuss about" anything. You discuss the price, discuss the schedule, discuss the problem. Adding "about" is a direct transfer from other languages and it marks you as non-native immediately. I still catch myself typing it and have to delete it.

The other one I see weekly is "I am waiting for your reply" when the writer means "I look forward to your reply." The first sounds like a complaint. The second sounds like a professional. A buyer in Germany once told me my "waiting for your reply" email felt like pressure. He was right. I rewrote it and the relationship improved.

## What I do now

I keep a short list taped inside my notebook, not on my desk where clients might see it. The list has maybe fifteen items: reply not revert, please not kindly, discuss without about, look forward to not waiting for. Before I send anything to a new contact, I read the email out loud once. That catches most of the tone problems.

The real cost is not the grammar itself. It is the moment the reader stops thinking about your offer and starts thinking about your English. That moment is expensive. A clean, simple email keeps the attention where it belongs: on the shipment date, the unit price, the delivery terms. Everything else is noise you pay for.
