Last Tuesday afternoon I sat at my desk staring at a reply from a supplier. The email said: "We will try our best to solve your problem." I had written to them about a shipment that arrived two weeks late, and I needed a concrete date for the replacement parts. Their reply felt polite but empty. I spent twenty minutes drafting a follow-up, then deleted it twice. The problem was not their English. The problem was the thinking behind the English.

## The mistake I kept making in my own writing

A year ago I would have written the same sentence. In Chinese, saying "我们会尽力解决您的问题" sounds responsible and warm. You acknowledge the issue and promise effort. But when you translate that directly into English, the reader hears something else. "We will try our best" sounds like a hedge. It sounds like you are preparing the customer for failure. I learned this the hard way when a client in Germany replied to my "we will try our best to meet the deadline" with a short line: "Please confirm the exact date. Try is not a plan."

I lost that order. Not because my product was bad, but because my language carried a Chinese logic that the client read as evasive. Direct translation of polite formulas is one of the biggest traps in foreign trade communication. In Chinese business culture, softening a commitment shows humility. In English business culture, that same softener reads as lack of confidence.

## Why direct translation fails even when grammar is correct

The grammar in "we will try our best" is perfect. The word order is fine. The verb tense is fine. But the sentence still fails. That is the core problem with Chinese to English direct translation in foreign trade: the sentence can be grammatically correct and still communicate the wrong message.

I see this every week in emails from Chinese suppliers. "Please kindly check the attached file." The word "kindly" is a direct carryover from "请查收附件". But in English, "kindly" in that position sounds old-fashioned and slightly annoyed. "Please see attached" or "Attached is the file you requested" is cleaner and more professional.

Another one: "We are sorry for the inconvenience caused to you." In Chinese, "给您带来不便深感抱歉" is a standard polite apology. But in English, the passive phrasing "inconvenience caused to you" sounds bureaucratic. A native English speaker in a business setting would more likely write: "I am sorry for the delay" or "We apologize for the trouble." The difference is small, but the direct translation carries a stiffness that makes the writer sound less competent.

## The fix starts with thinking about the reader's expectation

The real fix is not memorizing better phrases. It is changing the order of thinking. In Chinese, I used to think: what do I want to say? Then I would translate that into English. Now I think: what does the reader need to hear, and how would a native English speaker in that role say it?

For example, when a shipment is late, a Chinese supplier might write: "The goods are delayed due to the bad weather. Please understand." The phrase "please understand" is a direct translation of "请理解". But in English, asking the customer to understand your problem puts the burden on them. A better approach is to state the fact and then give the customer something actionable: "The shipment was delayed by storms at the port. The new estimated arrival date is Friday. I will send tracking details as soon as the vessel departs."

That version does not ask for understanding. It gives information and a next step. The customer feels respected, not burdened.

## What I do now before sending any English email

I read my draft out loud. If a sentence sounds like something I would say in Chinese and then translated, I stop and rewrite it. I ask myself: is there a shorter, more direct way to say this? Does the sentence give the reader a clear next action? Am I using a polite formula that hides a lack of information?

I also stopped using "maybe" and "perhaps" in messages to customers. In Chinese, "可能" and "也许" soften a statement and make it sound less aggressive. In English, they make you sound unsure of your own facts. If I do not know something, I say "I will check and get back to you by tomorrow." That is honest and direct, and it builds more trust than a vague "maybe next week."

Foreign trade communication is not about translating words. It is about translating intent across two different business cultures. The sooner you stop translating sentences and start translating meaning, the fewer misunderstandings you will have. I still make mistakes. But now I catch them before the customer does.
