In my first year of foreign trade work, every email I sent looked the same. Dear Sir. We are pleased to inform you that the goods have been shipped. Please feel free to contact us if you have any questions. Every sentence was grammatically correct, copied from textbooks and online templates.

For a while I thought this was what professional writing looked like. Then an American customer replied to one of my long, formal emails with a single line: Got it, thanks!

I stared at the screen for a few seconds. He wrote like a person talking. I wrote like a notice being read aloud. We were discussing the same shipment, but we were not in the same register. After that, I started paying attention to how real people write emails, and I went back through my old sentences one by one.

## We are pleased to inform you

This was the first phrase I dropped. The problem is not that it is wrong, it is that it says nothing. You are informing someone about something, so the word inform is redundant, and pleased adds a layer of ceremony before any actual content.

Real emails just say the thing: Your order shipped today. Or with a softer entry: Quick update: the goods left the factory this morning. Short, but it carries information. Politeness is not banned, it just fits in one word, Hi.

## Please be advised that

I used this one in formal notices for a long time before I noticed it belongs almost exclusively in legal documents. When I wrote Please be advised that the price is valid for 30 days, a native speaker would simply write The price is valid for 30 days.

The phrase adds words without adding meaning. Cut it and the sentence loses nothing.

## Kindly do the needful

Many people pick this one up from South Asian customers, then pass it along in their own emails. Do the needful literally means do what needs to be done, which leaves the other side guessing what that is. Kindly also reads as stiff, almost bossy, to American and British readers.

I now spell out the action instead: Could you send the updated PI by Friday? What I need, and by when, in one sentence. Making the reader guess your request is the most expensive habit in business email.

## Please revert

I picked up revert from email exchanges with Indian customers, where Please revert at the earliest appears constantly and looks perfectly natural. For a while I assumed it was standard English. Then I checked a dictionary: revert means to return to a previous state, not to reply. The normal words are reply, or get back to me.

What interests me is that this usage is alive within its own community. There is no single standard English; different circles have different conventions. But you should know which layer you are writing in. Using revert with an Indian customer works fine. Using it with an American customer can cause confusion. Knowing the history and choosing deliberately is different from copying blindly.

## Thanks for your kind reply

The kind in kind reply belongs to the same family. A real person writes Thanks for the reply, or Thanks for getting back to me. Adding kind is like opening every sentence in Chinese with a formal honorific. Too much politeness creates distance.

Politeness in email comes from being short and direct, not from stacking adjectives.

## How I check myself now

After writing an email, I read it silently and listen to it. If it sounds like a person talking, it stays. If it sounds like a notice, a contract, or a template, I rewrite it. The test is simple: would I say this sentence out loud if we were talking face to face?

Another rule: split long sentences instead of stacking clauses. I used to write things like The samples which were sent to you last week should have reached you by now. Now I write: The samples left last week. You should have them by now. Email is not a writing contest. Short sentences are faster to read, and your customer is reading dozens of them a day.

For English learners stuck at the intermediate level, the problem is often not vocabulary. It is the unwillingness to write short, direct sentences. It took me a long time to accept that writing simply in email is not a sign of weak English. It is a way of saving the other person's time.
