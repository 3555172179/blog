The invoice sat in my drafts folder for nine days. I had quoted a freight forwarder for a consolidated shipment, and the silence was starting to feel personal. Every time I opened the thread to nudge them, I typed a sentence, deleted it, and closed the tab. The problem was never the words themselves. It was the fear that any follow-up would read as impatience, and impatience kills deals faster than a bad rate.

## The real cost of a bad follow-up

I once sent a follow-up that cost me a supplier relationship. A factory had gone quiet for two weeks on a sample request. I wrote back: "Any update?" Two words. The reply came within an hour, but it was cold. They apologized for the delay, said they were busy with other orders, and asked if I could wait another month. I never got the samples. Looking back, my message did nothing to help them prioritize me. It only signaled that I was watching the clock.

That experience taught me something I still use now. A follow-up is not a reminder of your deadline. It is a chance to lower the other person's effort to respond.

## Make the reply the easiest thing they do that day

The draft that finally worked for the freight forwarder did not ask for a decision. It gave them an off-ramp. I wrote that I was updating my shipment schedule for the next two weeks and wanted to confirm whether their quote was still valid, or whether they would prefer I look at alternative routing. No pressure, no guilt. Just a clear choice: confirm or release.

They replied the next morning. The quote was still good, and they apologized for the delay because a colleague had been on leave. The deal closed three days later.

The difference between "Any update?" and that message is not tone. It is structure. A good follow-up removes the social cost of having gone silent. It lets the other person save face and move forward without explaining themselves.

## One useful rule for timing

I used to follow up after three days no matter what. Now I match the interval to the original promise. If someone says they will get back to me by Friday, I write on Monday morning. If they gave no timeline, I wait seven to ten days before the first nudge. Anything sooner reads as anxiety. Anything later reads as disinterest.

For the freight forwarder, I waited nine days because they had said "early next week" and then vanished. By the time I wrote, enough days had passed that my message felt like a practical check, not a tap on the shoulder.

## What I no longer write

I stopped using phrases like "just checking in" and "bumping this to the top of your inbox." They sound polite, but they carry an undercurrent of nagging. Instead, I lead with new information or a specific question that is easy to answer. Even a one-line message can work if it gives the recipient a reason to reply that is about them, not about my waiting.

A follow-up email is a small piece of writing, but it carries real weight. Send it wrong and you look desperate. Send it right and you look organized, patient, and easy to work with. That is the reputation I want in international trade, where every silent inbox is a test of judgment.
