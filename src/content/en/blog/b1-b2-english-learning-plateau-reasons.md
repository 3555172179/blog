I had read the same paragraph about supply chain logistics three times. I understood every word, but when I closed the document and tried to explain the main idea out loud, my mouth produced something like, "The thing is, companies need to, you know, move stuff better." That was the moment I admitted it: my English has been parked at B2 for about four years, and I have been doing almost nothing that actually moves it.

I am a foreign trade practitioner. I write emails every day, join video calls, and read contracts in English. On paper, that sounds like full immersion. But my daily English is a closed loop. I use the same 800 words, the same three tenses, and the same polite phrases. My clients understand me. My boss does not complain. So my brain decided this level is good enough, and it stopped paying the cost of improvement.

## The comfort zone is a vocabulary prison

Here is what I noticed when I started recording myself on calls. I avoid any structure I cannot produce automatically. If I want to say something hypothetical, I dodge it. Instead of "If we had shipped earlier, we would have avoided the delay," I say "We shipped late, so delay." Grammatically broken, but the meaning lands. The client nods. I survive. And my B2 fossilizes a little more.

The problem is not lack of exposure. I consume English content every day: news, podcasts, YouTube videos about website building. But consumption is passive. Understanding a native speaker does not train my mouth or my writing hand. B1/B2 learners are excellent at decoding. We are terrible at encoding. That gap is exactly where the plateau lives.

## Grammar study without output is just entertainment

I bought a grammar workbook in March. I did 40 pages of conditionals and reported speech. I scored well on the exercises. Then on a call with a supplier, I said, "He told me the shipment will arrive next week." A textbook error. My brain knew the rule. My mouth ignored it.

That failure cost me a small misunderstanding. The supplier corrected me politely, and I felt the familiar shame of knowing better but doing worse. I realized that grammar exercises give the illusion of progress. They make you feel studious while your real-time speech stays exactly the same. The only fix is forced output with feedback. I started writing a short daily voice note about one business topic, then listening back and marking every place where I simplified or dodged a structure I supposedly know.

## The intermediate plateau is a feedback problem

For years I had no one correcting me. My clients are not English teachers. They care about the order, not my articles. My colleagues are also non-native speakers. We all reinforce each other's mistakes. When I finally hired an online tutor for two sessions a month, she flagged things I had been saying wrong for a decade: "discuss about," "according to me," and a weird habit of using "already" in present tense for future plans.

Those mistakes were invisible to me because they never caused a breakdown in communication. But they mark me as B2. Breaking the plateau means seeking out that uncomfortable feedback. I now ask one specific question at the end of every tutoring session: "What is one thing I said today that a C1 speaker would not say?" The answers hurt. They also work.

## What I changed this month

I stopped reading articles just to understand them. Now I read one short article, close it, and write a 100-word summary without looking back. Then I compare my version to the original and note every place where my sentence structure collapsed into something simpler. I also record a 2-minute monologue three times a week about my work, then transcribe it by hand. Seeing my own broken sentences on paper is brutal, but it is the most honest mirror I have found.

That supply chain passage is still somewhere in my browser history. But last night I explained its main idea out loud in four connected sentences, with one conditional and one passive construction. It was not elegant. It was not fast. But it was B2 pushing upward, and that is the only direction that matters.
