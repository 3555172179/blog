The freight forwarder's email said the container would miss the cutoff by two days. The buyer would probably ask for a discount, or cancel part of the order. I felt my face get warm and typed out a reply: "This is unacceptable, we agreed on the schedule weeks ago." Then I stopped. I remembered the rule I had been trying to follow for about a year: write it down, wait until tomorrow, then judge.

## The afternoon I almost sent a bad reply

I saved the draft, opened a plain text file on my desktop, and wrote three lines. First, what happened: the forwarder said the vessel schedule changed and the container would be late. Second, what I felt: angry and blamed them for not warning me earlier. Third, what I wanted to do: send the angry email, demand compensation, and copy my manager. Then I closed the file and went to work on something else.

The next morning I read my note again. The anger had faded a little. I looked at the forwarder's original message more carefully and saw they had included a note two weeks earlier about possible port congestion. I had missed it because I was busy with a website migration. So the delay was not entirely their fault, and my angry email would have made me look careless. Instead I wrote a calmer message asking for the earliest possible alternative sailing and a revised arrival estimate. The buyer accepted the new date without penalty.

## Why immediate judgment fails in trade work

In foreign trade, many problems feel urgent because money and relationships are involved. A supplier sends a wrong sample. A customer complains about packaging. A payment is delayed by a bank holiday. My first reaction is usually to judge quickly and respond quickly, because I think speed shows professionalism. But speed often means I react to the emotion of the moment, not to the facts.

The habit of writing a note and waiting one day works because it separates two things: recording the event and judging the event. Recording is fast and safe. Judging is slow and should be delayed. When I write down what happened and what I felt, I am not ignoring the problem. I am putting it in a holding area. The next day I can look at it with less adrenaline.

## How I use the note in website and learning tasks

The same method helps with website building and English learning, which are part of my daily work. If I change a page layout and the traffic drops the next day, my first thought is "I broke something." I write that down and wait. Often the drop is seasonal or related to a search engine update that also hit other pages. If I had reverted the change immediately, I would have lost useful data.

With English, I keep a small notebook for phrases I hear in meetings or read in trade articles. When I see a phrase like "subject to final confirmation" or "without prejudice," I write it down with a guess about the meaning. I do not check the dictionary that same day. I wait until the next morning. My memory of the guess becomes stronger, and the correction sticks better because I compared my wrong guess with the right meaning.

## The cost of waiting is smaller than I feared

I used to worry that waiting a day would make me seem slow or unresponsive. In practice, one day rarely changes the outcome of a trade problem. A shipment delay, a quality complaint, or a website bug can usually wait 24 hours for a more careful reply. The only exceptions are true emergencies like a container fire or a legal deadline, and those are rare.

The note file on my desktop now has hundreds of entries. Some are one line, some are a short paragraph. I do not organize them by date or category. I just scroll through them sometimes and see patterns in my own overreactions. The method costs me almost nothing, but it has saved me from sending many emails I would have regretted and from making website changes based on panic instead of evidence.

Last week I opened the file and saw an entry from six months ago about a customer who asked for a price reduction on a repeat order. My first reaction then was "they are testing my margin." I waited a day and replied with a smaller reduction tied to a larger quantity. The customer agreed. If I had replied the same day, I might have given a bigger discount or refused outright. The note did not make the decision for me. It just gave me enough distance to see the request as a negotiation, not an attack.
