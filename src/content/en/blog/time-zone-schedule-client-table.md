Last Tuesday I sat down at 11:40 pm with a cup of cold coffee and opened my inbox. A client in Brazil had sent three messages between 2 pm and 4 pm his time. I replied at midnight my time, which was early evening for him. He answered immediately, then asked why I always seemed to work at strange hours. I did not have a good answer.

That night I realized my schedule was not a schedule. It was a reaction loop. I checked my phone whenever I woke up, whenever I ate, whenever I could not sleep. Some clients got replies within minutes. Others waited a day because their morning was my middle of the night. I had no map of who was awake when.

## Building the First Table

I opened a blank spreadsheet the next morning. Across the top I put my own working hours in UTC, 7 am to 7 pm. Down the side I listed the countries where I had active clients. For each country I wrote the offset from UTC. Then I colored the cells where my working hours overlapped with a reasonable business day in that country, say 9 am to 6 pm local time.

The first version was useless. I had ten countries in one column, and the overlap cells looked like a checkerboard. I could not tell at a glance whether I should call a client in Turkey before lunch or after dinner. The table had data but no decision.

## Splitting Clients by Urgency

I redrew the table two days later. This time I grouped clients by how quickly they expected a response. One group was same-day, another was within 24 hours, and a third was flexible. I placed those groups as rows under each country. Then I assigned a two-hour block in my day to each group, based on the time zone overlap.

For example, clients in Western Europe got my early afternoon block. Their morning was already underway, and my replies arrived before their lunch. Clients in Southeast Asia got my late evening block, which was their next morning. South American clients got my mid-morning, which was their mid-afternoon. I wrote those blocks into the table as bold borders, not just colors.

## The Cost of Getting It Wrong

The first week I followed the table strictly. I missed a request from a client in Mexico because I had lumped all of Latin America into one row. Mexico City is one hour behind Colombia and two hours behind parts of Brazil. I replied six hours late to a shipping question that needed a same-day answer. The client did not complain, but I could hear the delay in his next message.

I fixed that by splitting Latin America into three rows: Mexico, Colombia and Peru, and Brazil. Each got its own overlap block. I also added a note column for daylight saving changes, because a client in Australia shifted by one hour in October and my table did not know it.

## Making the Table Live on My Desk

I printed the final table on a single page and taped it next to my monitor. The paper version has coffee stains now. I update it by hand when a new client appears or when a country changes its clock. The spreadsheet version lives in my phone, but I rarely open it. The paper is faster.

At 11:40 pm last night I did not check my inbox. I looked at the table. Brazil was in my mid-morning block tomorrow. I closed the laptop and went to bed. This morning I answered his three messages at 9:15 am my time, which was 10:15 am in São Paulo. He replied within ten minutes. No one asked why I was working at a strange hour.
